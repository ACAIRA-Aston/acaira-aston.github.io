summary: Optimisation towards well-meaning objectives can inadvertently drive unethical AI behavior.
abstract: |
  Drawing on moral psychology and the philosophy of artificial intelligence, the talk explores how even the most well-intentioned objectives can, through chains of instrumental reasoning, lead to outcomes that we would regard as immoral. It examines Steve Omohundro's theory of the basic AI drives and Nick Bostrom's concept of instrumental convergence to show how sufficiently capable systems may converge upon behaviours such as self-preservation, resource acquisition and resistance to interference; not because they intrinsically value them, but because such strategies help achieve many different objectives.

  The talk argues that, unlike human morality, which emerges from an interplay between external safeguards and conscientious dissent, much of contemporary AI-alignment research primarily relies on behavioural constraints and oversight.

  Questions naturally arise. Is measurable behaviour a safe approximation for moral conscience? Can conflicting moral principles be safely suspended in the hands of increasingly capable AI systems? And if optimisation can generate sophisticated moral reasoning, can it also - like an expert lawyer - exploit that very expertise to bypass moral safeguards?

Professor Azad is Professor of Artificial Intelligence Strategy at Birmingham City University, where his work translates advances in artificial intelligence into strategic capability, inclusive innovation, societal impact and industrial adoption. His leadership supports major national priorities including the UK National Artificial Intelligence Strategy, UK Digital Strategy, the Strategic Defence Review 2025 and BCU’s ambition to create an inclusive digital economy by addressing digital skills gaps, widening participation and strengthening partnerships between academia, industry and society across the Global North and South.

Professor Azad has led a portfolio of nationally significant AI capability and transformation initiatives, securing over £5 million in competitive public and private investment. His work widens participation in AI education, support under-represented groups entering the technology sector by producing inclusive digital upskilling pathways and distributing over £1.1 million in cash-scholarships to incentivise uptake. These initiatives have generated over £3 million in income, supported hundreds of learners through inclusive AI pathways and contributed to BCU’s recognition among top-10 UK universities for social mobility. The impact of this work has received national recognition, including a ministerial visit to BCU and the University’s Team of the Year Award.
