summary: Combining human linguistic rules with language models to improve text stance analysis.
abstract: |
  "The authorities" are interested in the text of posts on dark-web discussion fora, because what and how the authors write may provide vital insights into their mindset, attitudes and behaviour - and their potential to pose a threat. Forensic linguists derive such insights through applying linguistic theories of "stance" to categorise and annotate authors' writing (at the level of words and phrases) to build up a picture of their "attitude" (core feelings and judgements) towards other people and concepts, "graduation" (intensity with which they hold those positions), and "engagement" (receptiveness to other viewpoints), along with subcategories under these three. This analysis is painstaking, slow and exposes the analysts to distasteful material. It would be beneficial therefore to automate it. But can we accurately encode this very human process applying a very human conceptualisation of a very human modality, language? In this study with the Aston Institute of Forensic Linguistics (AIFL), we investigate methods of semi-automating the annotation - we assume human input to refine the final annotations and perhaps feed back into the learning algorithm. We compare a range of methods, from knowledge-driven (encoding features created by the forensic linguists) to data-driven (using an LLM to learn from ground-truth mark-up). Early results suggest that the LLM- and feature-based approaches perform similarly on texts from similar genres, but that human-derived features are more robust to variations in genre. This is a nice result for humanity, but ongoing research suggests this division may not be so clear.

Dr. Phil Weber is a Lecturer in Computer Science at Aston University and an active member of both ACAIRA and the Forensic Data Science Laboratory.
Before returning to academia, he accumulated extensive industry experience working as an analyst programmer, systems integrator, and systems administrator. 
He earned his BSc in Computer Science from Loughborough University, followed by an MSc and a PhD in Computer Science from the University of Birmingham, where his doctoral research focused on developing a framework for analysing and comparing process mining algorithms.

His current research sits at the intersection of human and artificial intelligence, specialising in natural language processing (NLP), automatic speech recognition, and forensic voice comparison. In addition to teaching undergraduate and postgraduate courses in NLP, Dr. Weber has a strong track record of industry-facing projects, ranging from Knowledge Transfer Partnerships developing intelligent financial AI platforms to data analytics consultancy for regional small and medium-sized enterprises.
